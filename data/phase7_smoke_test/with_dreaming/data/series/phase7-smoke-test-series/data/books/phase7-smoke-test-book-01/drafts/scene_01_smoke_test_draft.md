The coffee shop smelled like caramel and ambition. Emma sat at the corner table, her laptop open to a half-finished elevation drawing, three reference books stacked beside her like a fortress. She'd been there for two hours, and her cappuccino had gone cold an hour ago. The Meridian project wasn't going to redesign itself, and she had exactly forty-eight hours before the client presentation.

She reached for her cup without looking, her eyes still fixed on the screen, fingers poised over the trackpad. The ceramic was warm enough to be recent. She didn't remember ordering a second one, but that was fine. Emma operated on a principle of efficient multitasking—work could happen anywhere, even with mediocre coffee.

The cup was halfway to her lips when someone pulled out the chair across from her.

"Sorry, is this—" the man started, but Emma was already mid-sip, already turning, already surprised by the intrusion into her carefully constructed work bubble.

Her hand jerked. The cup tilted. The universe tilted with it.

Coffee cascaded across the table in a brown arc that seemed to move in slow motion, and Emma watched in horror as it made contact with the large portfolio that had been resting against the opposite chair. The portfolio that, she would later learn, contained months of work.

"No, no, no—" she gasped, jumping up so fast her own chair scraped backward with a screech.

The man moved faster. He grabbed the portfolio, pulling it back, but the damage was already spreading across the white papers inside like a dark stain. His face cycled through surprise to dismay to something that looked dangerously close to devastation.

"I am so sorry," Emma said, the words tumbling out in a rush. She was already grabbing napkins from the dispenser on the next table, knocking over the small vase of flowers in her haste. "Oh God, I didn't see you, I didn't—"

"It's okay," he said, but his voice had gone tight. He was looking at the portfolio the way someone looks at a wounded animal. "It's fine."

It was absolutely not fine. Emma could see that clearly. The papers were blueprints—architectural blueprints, which made the cosmic irony of the situation even more painful. She knew blueprints. She knew how long they took to produce, how precious each line was, how they represented not just work but vision, intention, hours of thought crystallized into paper.

"I'm so sorry," she said again, pressing napkins against the wet pages. "These are—what are these?"

"Restaurant designs," he said quietly. He was still holding the portfolio, watching her dab at the papers with the intensity of someone watching their house burn down. "I was going to show them to an investor tomorrow."

Emma's stomach dropped. "Tomorrow? Oh God. I—" She looked at his face properly for the first time, and found herself momentarily distracted by the fact that he was attractive in that effortless way that made her immediately suspicious of her own judgment. Dark hair, sharp cheekbones, eyes that were currently filled with a mixture of resignation and barely suppressed panic. "What's your name?"

"Marcus."

"Emma. And Marcus, I will fix this. I will absolutely fix this. Do you have digital files?"

"Yeah, but—" He looked down at the blueprints again, at the coffee-stained corners, the way the ink was starting to run on one of the sketches. "These were the originals. I had them printed on special paper, wanted to show the investor the tactile thing, you know? The weight of it, the quality. It's supposed to mean something."

Emma understood this completely. She also understood that she had just destroyed something that meant something, and the guilt was a physical thing, sitting heavy in her chest.

"I can help," she heard herself say. "I'm an architect. I can help you recreate these, or restore them, or—" She was already thinking through the possibilities, the layers of the problem. "How long do you have?"

Marcus looked at her like she'd offered him a winning lottery ticket and a puppy. "You'd do that?"

"I spilled coffee on your blueprints," Emma said flatly. "I would do considerably more than that."

He laughed then—a short, surprised sound that caught in his throat. "You're serious."

"Completely serious. Also, I'm going to buy you a new coffee, and I'm going to sit here and help you dry these out properly, and then I'm going to get your contact information and we're going to figure out a timeline." She was already moving, already in problem-solving mode, already feeling the familiar thrill of a challenge that wasn't her own project. "What kind of restaurant?"

"Farm-to-table," Marcus said, and something shifted in his expression. The devastation receded, replaced by something warmer. "Mediterranean influence. The investor wants to turn an old warehouse into something with character, something that tells a story."

"Show me," Emma said, pulling out a chair and sitting down across from him. "Show me what you were going to show them."

He hesitated for only a moment before opening the portfolio fully, carefully extracting the least-damaged blueprints. His hands were careful, reverent. As he spread them out, Emma saw what he'd been trying to communicate—not just a restaurant, but a space, an experience. The design had a flow to it, a narrative arc that moved diners through the building like a story unfolding.

"This is good," she said, and meant it. "This is really good."

"Yeah?" He looked at her like her opinion actually mattered, and Emma felt something shift in her chest. Something that had nothing to do with guilt and everything to do with the fact that he was looking at her with genuine interest now, not annoyance.

"Yeah," she confirmed. "And it's fixable. Most of it, anyway. The ink is good quality—it's not going to bleed much more. We can scan these, get them restored digitally, reprint them on the same paper stock." She was already pulling out her phone, already making mental notes. "When's your meeting?"

"Tomorrow at three."

"Then we have tonight," Emma said. "What's your number?"

He gave it to her, and she texted him immediately so he'd have hers. Their eyes met across the table, and Emma felt that spark—that unmistakable moment of recognition that sometimes happened between people. The kind that made you wonder if the universe had a sense of humor.

"For what it's worth," Marcus said slowly, "this is the most interesting way I've ever met someone."

Emma laughed. "I have a feeling you say that to all the women who destroy your life's work."

"Only the ones who offer to fix it," he said, and smiled.
