The espresso machine hissed like a irritated cat as Emma slid into the corner booth at Meridian Coffee, her laptop bag already open, blueprints half-unfurled across the scarred wooden table. She'd been coming here for three weeks now—ever since the Holloway project had consumed her life—and the barista knew her order by heart: double shot, oat milk, no sugar. Efficiency. She didn't have time for pleasantries.

She spread the blueprints wider, her fingers tracing the revised load-bearing walls for the residential conversion. The client had rejected her first proposal, something about "not enough character," which was corporate speak for "we want you to make magic happen with a budget that couldn't buy magic a decent lunch." Emma's jaw tightened. She'd been awake until 2 AM redrawing these, and the lines still weren't right.

The coffee arrived. She didn't look up.

Marcus was thinking about beurre blanc—specifically, whether he could get the emulsification right without the temperature spiking—when he noticed the woman in the corner booth. Not in a romantic way, not yet. He noticed her the way he noticed ingredients: the sharp cut of her dark hair, the intensity of her focus, the way she held a pencil like it owed her money. He'd been coming to Meridian for six months, sketching out his restaurant concept on napkins and the backs of takeout menus, but he'd never seen her before.

He sat two tables away, his own papers spread before him—architectural drawings, actually, which struck him as funny. His restaurant was going to be in a converted warehouse, and he'd hired an architect to handle the structural stuff, but he still liked to sketch the vision himself. The layout. The flow. The way light would hit the kitchen pass at 6 PM when service started.

Emma reached for her coffee without looking. Her fingers found the cup, lifted it, brought it to her lips.

That's when Marcus knocked over his water glass.

Not on himself. On the floor. But the shock of it—the clatter, the splash—made him jerk sideways in his chair, and his elbow caught the edge of the table, and suddenly his entire stack of papers was sliding sideways like a landslide, and he was lunging for them with both hands, and—

Emma's coffee went flying.

It happened in that strange slow-motion way that disasters do. She watched the cup leave her hand. Watched the arc of it. Watched the brown liquid spray across the table like a wave hitting a seawall. And then she watched it rain down on the man who was now standing up, papers everywhere, his face a mixture of panic and mortification.

"Oh my god," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a statement of fact. A prayer. A curse.

The coffee had caught him across the chest and shoulder, soaking into what looked like an expensive linen shirt. But worse—so much worse—it had caught his papers. His blueprints. They were already curling at the edges, the ink running like mascara down a crying face.

"I'm so sorry," Emma said, and she meant it with every fiber of her being. She was on her feet, napkins in hand, moving without thinking. "I'm so sorry, I wasn't even—I was just reaching for—"

"It's fine," Marcus said, but his voice cracked slightly on the word "fine," and they both knew it wasn't fine. He was dabbing at the papers with the inadequate napkins she'd provided, watching his work dissolve. "It's okay. Accidents happen."

"No, this is—" Emma grabbed more napkins from the dispenser, her hands moving with the same precise efficiency she applied to everything else. "This is my fault. I wasn't paying attention. I'm never not paying attention. I don't know what I was—"

"Really, it's okay." He looked up at her then, and there was something in his eyes that made her stop mid-apology. Not anger. Resignation, maybe. Or the kind of humor that only emerges when you're standing in the wreckage of something you've spent hours creating. "I'm Marcus."

"Emma." She was still holding napkins like they might somehow reverse the laws of physics. "I can pay for—whatever this is. The shirt. The papers. I can have them professionally restored, or I can pay you to redo them, or—"

"Did you spill coffee on them on purpose?" He was smiling now, actually smiling, which somehow made it worse.

"No, of course not."

"Then it's an accident. A very wet, very unfortunate accident, but an accident." He looked down at his ruined blueprints, then back at her. "Though I have to say, if you were trying to get my attention, there were probably easier ways."

Despite everything—the guilt, the adrenaline, the way her face was definitely turning red—Emma felt her mouth twitch. "I wasn't trying to get your attention."

"Really? Because you've been sitting here for three weeks, and you've never once looked up from whatever you're doing." He gestured to her table, where her own blueprints were somehow miraculously dry. "And now suddenly you're launching beverages across the room like some kind of caffeinated missile."

"I wasn't—" She stopped. He was teasing her. This stranger whose papers she'd destroyed was teasing her. "That's not fair."

"Fair?" He laughed, actually laughed, a warm sound that seemed to fill the small coffee shop. "You just destroyed three hours of work, and you're worried about fair?"

The barista appeared with a towel and a look of practiced sympathy. Emma used the distraction to study him properly for the first time. He was tall, with the kind of easy confidence that came from knowing exactly what you were doing. His dark curly hair was slightly damp from the water he'd spilled, and there was a smudge of what looked like pencil lead on his jaw. He was handsome in an unfinished way—like he was still becoming himself.

"Let me buy you a new coffee," she said. "And lunch. And I'll pay to have your papers restored, or—"

"What if you let me buy you a coffee instead?" He was wringing out the corner of a blueprint, which was probably pointless, but he seemed committed to the effort. "And then you can tell me what you're working on over there that's so important you don't notice when you're actively committing acts of beverage violence."

Emma should have said no. She had a meeting in forty minutes. The Holloway project was due in three days. She didn't have time for coffee with attractive strangers who made her laugh even as she was destroying their life's work.

"Okay," she said instead. "But I'm buying."

His smile widened. "Now we're negotiating. I like that."

And somewhere between the ruined blueprints and the second round of coffee, Emma stopped thinking about load-bearing walls and started noticing the way Marcus's hands moved when he talked about food, the same way hers moved when she talked about space and light. She noticed that his sketches, even soaked and running, showed a real understanding of flow and function.

He noticed that she was brilliant and driven and that she'd genuinely felt terrible about the coffee, which said something about her character.

Neither of them mentioned that sometimes the best things in life happen when you're not paying attention.
