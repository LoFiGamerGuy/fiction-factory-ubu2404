The private table at Meridian sat in the corner of the kitchen's viewing window—Marcus's favorite spot. From here, you could see everything: the controlled chaos of the line, the flames, the precision. Emma arrived at seven, and Marcus felt something shift in his chest the moment she crossed the threshold.

She wore a charcoal dress that caught the light, her dark hair pulled back to expose the line of her neck. Simple. Elegant. It was the kind of choice that suggested thought, not accident.

"You came," Marcus said, standing to greet her. Stupid thing to say. Of course she came.

"You asked," Emma replied, and there was something in her smile that made him feel seen in a way he wasn't used to.

The first course arrived—a delicate arrangement of uni, citrus, and microgreens that Marcus had designed that afternoon. He watched her face as she tasted it, the small pause before her eyes closed.

"This is..." she started, then shook her head. "I don't have words."

"Try," he said, leaning forward slightly. "Tell me what you taste."

Emma set down her fork, considering. "Umami. But underneath it, there's this brightness. Like the ocean, but clean. Like morning." She looked at him. "That's intentional, isn't it? The contrast."

Marcus felt his shoulders relax. "Yes. Everyone tastes the salt and the sea. But there's a moment before that—a space where your palate resets. I wanted to build that into the dish."

"You're designing," Emma said quietly. "With food."

"You do that with buildings."

It wasn't a question, and something passed between them—a recognition. Emma picked up her wine, and Marcus noticed her fingers were long, capable. The hand of someone who created things.

"I do," she admitted. "Though I'm not sure I'm very good at it anymore."

The vulnerability in that statement hung between them. Marcus didn't push. Instead, he said, "Tell me about your last project."

Emma's expression shifted—something like pain, quickly masked. But she started talking anyway, and as she did, her hands came alive, gesturing, building invisible structures in the air between them.

"It was a community center in the Midwest. A small town that had lost its downtown to sprawl. The client wanted to revitalize the core." Her eyes brightened as she spoke. "I designed it around this central atrium—all glass and light—with the idea that it would become a gathering place. The town could see itself reflected in the building."

"Could they?" Marcus asked.

Emma's hands stilled. "No. The budget got cut. They removed most of the glass, added more walls. It became... functional. Practical. Everything I didn't want it to be." She took a long drink of wine. "I haven't designed anything since."

Marcus understood something about that—the gap between vision and execution, the way the world could hollow out your dreams. He'd been there, in kitchens where he was told to cut corners, to use cheaper ingredients, to prioritize profit over passion.

"Why not?" he asked.

"Because I realized I wasn't very good at fighting for the things that mattered," Emma said, and there was real pain in her voice now. "And I wasn't sure if the things that mattered to me actually mattered to anyone else."

The second course arrived—a small plate of scallop with brown butter and sage. But Marcus barely noticed the server. He was watching Emma's face, the way she'd withdrawn slightly into herself.

"I think," he said slowly, "that you're looking at it wrong."

Emma's eyes met his, questioning.

"The building you designed—the one they compromised—that was still your design underneath. The intention was still there, even if they buried it." He gestured around the kitchen, visible through the glass. "I have a sous chef who tried to tell me last week that technique doesn't matter, that people just want to eat. But I know better. They might not be able to articulate it, but they taste the intention. They feel it."

"Do they?" Emma asked, and she sounded uncertain, almost desperate.

"Yes," Marcus said with certainty he didn't know he possessed. "I know they do. Because you just did. You tasted the design in that first course. You recognized intention because you're someone who creates with intention."

Emma looked down at her plate, and when she looked back up, there was something different in her eyes. Something that looked like the beginning of hope.

They ate slowly through the remaining courses. Marcus explained his philosophy—that every element had to earn its place, that simplicity was the hardest thing to achieve, that the most important ingredient was understanding the person you were cooking for. Emma shared sketches on her phone, buildings she'd designed, projects that never happened. There was a museum that existed only in her mind, a library that had been her thesis project, a private home that had been built but never quite the way she'd envisioned.

"Why do you carry these around?" Marcus asked, scrolling through her designs.

"Because they're proof," Emma said. "That I had ideas. That I was capable of having them."

"You still are."

"I don't know how to want that anymore," she whispered. "It hurt too much when it didn't work."

Marcus set down the phone and reached across the table. He didn't quite touch her hand, but his was close enough that she could feel the warmth of it. "Then design something just for yourself. Something with no client, no budget constraints, no compromise. Something that's only ever going to exist for you."

Emma's breath caught slightly. She looked at his hand, so close to hers, then back at his face. "Is that what you did? After the compromises?"

"I opened this place," Marcus said. "Every plate that leaves this kitchen is exactly what I intended. No compromises."

"That must be lonely," Emma said softly.

"It is," Marcus admitted. The honesty of it surprised him. "Until someone understands what you're trying to do. Until someone tastes it and gets it."

Emma's hand moved slightly, and her fingers brushed his. It was barely contact—a whisper of touch—but it felt like the most honest thing that had happened between them.

"I think," she said, "I might want to design something again."

Marcus turned his hand over, and this time, Emma didn't pull away.
