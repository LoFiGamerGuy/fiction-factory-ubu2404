The knock came at seven-fifteen on a Thursday, the kind of knock that made Emma's stomach drop before she even knew why. Through the peephole, she saw him—David, in a charcoal coat she'd never seen before, holding what looked like takeout from the Thai place they used to love. The one Marcus had never taken her to.

She didn't open the door immediately. Her hand stayed on the deadbolt while she breathed, counting the seconds like they were currency she was running out of.

"Em? I know you're home. Your car's in the lot."

Of course he knew where she lived. He'd helped her pick the apartment. They'd spent an entire Saturday looking at places, and she'd chosen this one because of the light in the bedroom. She'd been so certain then. So ready to build something.

Emma opened the door.

"Hey," David said, and his smile was exactly the way she remembered it—careful, hopeful, like he was always waiting for her to change her mind. "I was in the city for a conference. Thought I'd take a chance."

"You should have called."

"Would you have said yes?"

She wouldn't have. They both knew it. She stepped back anyway, let him inside, watched him catalog the changes she'd made since he'd last been here. The throw pillows. The new bookshelf. The absence of the engagement photos they'd taken at the botanical gardens.

"It looks good," he said. "You look good."

Emma crossed her arms. "What are you doing here, David?"

"I wanted to see if you were okay. Really okay, not the version you tell people." He set the takeout on her kitchen counter, and the smell of lemongrass and fish sauce filled the small space. "I heard through the grapevine that you're seeing someone."

The word *someone* hung in the air between them like smoke. Accusatory. Hurt.

"I am."

"Serious?"

"I don't know yet," Emma said, and the moment the words left her mouth, she felt them land wrong. Too honest. Too revealing. She could see David recalibrating, could watch him think that maybe, possibly, there was still a chance. "It's early."

"Early enough to figure out if you're making the same mistakes again?"

The question hit like a slap. "That's not fair."

"Isn't it?" David moved closer, and Emma didn't step back, which felt like its own kind of betrayal. "Em, you left me because you got scared. You were terrified of committing, and instead of working through it, you just... ran. And now you're doing this again with someone new, and I'm worried you're going to hurt him the same way you hurt me."

Emma's chest tightened. "I didn't hurt you. I was honest with you."

"After you'd already agreed to spend your life with me. After you'd picked out a dress and invited two hundred people to watch you promise forever." His voice cracked slightly. "I'm just saying, maybe you should figure out what you actually want before you drag someone else through this."

The words were barely out of his mouth when Emma heard her own door open. Marcus stood in the doorway, holding grocery bags, and she watched his face shift through confusion, recognition, and finally something that looked like betrayal.

"Oh," David said, turning to look at him. "You must be the someone."

Marcus set the bags down very carefully on the nearest surface. "Yeah. I am." His eyes found Emma's, and they were cold in a way she'd never seen before. "Who's this?"

"David. My ex-fiancé." She said it too quickly, too defensively, which made it worse. "He just showed up. I was about to ask him to leave."

But she hadn't been. They both knew she hadn't been.

Marcus nodded slowly, processing. "Right. Okay." He started to turn, and Emma felt something in her chest crack open.

"Marcus, wait—"

"I'm going to go," David said quietly. "I can see this isn't a good time." He looked at Emma with an expression that was somehow worse than anger—it was pity, mixed with something that looked like vindication. "Call me if you want to talk. Really talk, I mean. About what you're afraid of."

After he left, the apartment felt too small. Marcus moved around the kitchen with mechanical precision, unpacking groceries and putting them away with the kind of focus people used when they were trying not to explode.

"So that happened," Emma said.

"Yeah. It did." Marcus didn't look at her. "How long has he been in contact with you?"

"He hasn't. That was the first time I've seen him in over a year."

"But you didn't tell him to leave right away."

"I didn't know what to say. He just showed up—"

"Emma." Marcus finally looked at her, and his eyes were tired in a way that made her stomach hurt. "You didn't tell him to leave. You let him come in. You let him talk to you about your relationship, about me, and you didn't once tell him to get out."

"Because I was shocked. Because—"

"Because part of you wanted him there." It wasn't a question. "Or at least, part of you didn't want him to leave."

"That's not true."

"Isn't it? He comes in here, tells you you're making mistakes, that you're going to hurt me the same way you hurt him, and you just... stand there. You let him define what's happening between us." Marcus ran a hand through his hair, and Emma could see the hurt underneath the anger now. "I thought we were past this. I thought you were past this."

"I am," Emma said, but the words felt hollow. Because David had said something true, hadn't he? She was scared. She was always scared. And maybe Marcus could see it now, written all over her face.

"I don't know if you are," Marcus said quietly. "And I don't know if I can keep doing this if you're not sure."

Emma opened her mouth to argue, to convince him, to promise something she wasn't sure she could keep. But the words stuck in her throat, and in the silence between them, she could feel the ground shifting beneath their connection, uncertain and unstable.

"So what does that mean?" she asked finally.

Marcus looked away. "I don't know yet. I need to think about it."

He left the groceries on the counter and walked toward the bedroom, closing the door softly behind him. Emma stood alone in her kitchen, surrounded by the smell of lemongrass that David had brought, feeling like she'd just lost something she hadn't known how to hold.
