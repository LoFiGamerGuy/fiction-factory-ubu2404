The morning light caught the water just right, turning the bay into hammered silver. Emma sat at her usual corner table at Saltwater, a ceramic mug warming her hands, her phone propped against the sugar dispenser as she scrolled through client emails. She'd learned not to check work messages before her second coffee. It made her jittery, made her say things she'd have to apologize for later.

She didn't see him come in.

The cafe was half-full—a Saturday morning crowd of locals and tourists, the kind of place where you could disappear into yourself if you wanted to. Emma wanted to. She had a presentation Monday for a tech company's new headquarters, and the lead architect had already rejected her first three concepts. Too bold, he'd said. Too experimental. As if bold and experimental were character flaws.

The man at the next table spread something across his surface with the careful reverence of someone handling something precious. Emma registered only the edge of it—white paper, dark lines. Blueprints, maybe. She turned back to her phone.

Her coffee was perfect today. Not too hot, not too cool, that precise temperature where it tasted like possibility. She lifted the mug, already composing a scathing response to the tech company's feedback in her head, when a woman at the neighboring table stood up suddenly, her chair scraping back with a sound like a cry. She'd been turning to leave, and her bag—oversized, overstuffed, the kind that seemed to contain an entire life—caught the edge of Emma's elbow.

The physics were simple and terrible.

Emma's mug tipped. The coffee didn't arc gracefully. It didn't give anyone time to react. It simply poured, a brown flood across the white paper, soaking into the lines and notations with the speed of betrayal.

"Oh my God," Emma said.

The man looked up. He had dark eyes, the kind that seemed to catalogue everything in a glance, and right now they were cataloguing disaster. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"I—" Emma was already standing, already reaching for napkins from the dispenser, her brain moving faster than her hands. "I am so sorry, I'm so—"

"It's okay," he said, but he was already moving the papers, trying to salvage what could be salvaged, which appeared to be nothing. The coffee had soaked through to the second sheet. "It's fine, it's—"

"It's not fine." Emma was dabbing uselessly at the paper, making it worse. She stopped. "I'm making it worse. I'm sorry."

He looked at her then, really looked at her, and something shifted in his expression. Not anger. Something softer. Resignation, maybe, mixed with the kind of dark humor that comes from watching your day implode in real time.

"Well," he said, "this is one way to meet someone."

Emma felt heat climb her neck. "I'm Emma. And I'm an architect, which means I understand the value of blueprints, which makes this approximately a thousand times worse than if I'd just spilled coffee on anyone."

He smiled. It was a real smile, not the polite kind. "Marcus. And you're an architect?"

"Yes. And you're—"

"About to lose a very important meeting with my contractor." He looked at the papers again, then back at her. "These are for a restaurant. New location. I was supposed to walk through the design with him at ten."

Emma's stomach dropped. "It's nine-fifteen."

"Yeah."

She sat back down. She didn't mean to. Her body just decided that standing was no longer an option. "I'm sorry. I'm genuinely, deeply sorry."

Marcus pulled out the chair across from her and sat down. He set the ruined blueprints to the side with the careful precision of someone putting away grief. "You know what? I've been running on fumes for three weeks. Maybe the universe is telling me to take a breath."

"The universe is telling you I'm a disaster."

"That too." He flagged down the server. "Can I get a coffee? And maybe some perspective?" He looked back at Emma. "You really are an architect?"

"Really am."

"What kind of work do you do?"

Emma found herself answering, found herself describing the tech company project, the rejection, the feeling of being too much and not enough simultaneously. She didn't usually talk about work with strangers. She didn't usually talk to strangers at all, if she could help it. But something about the way he listened—the way he actually listened, not just waited for his turn to speak—made her keep going.

When she finished, he was quiet for a moment. Then: "Can I show you something?"

He pulled out his phone and scrolled through photos. They were of a space—raw, industrial, beautiful in its emptiness. Large windows overlooking the water. Exposed brick. Potential.

"This is the space," he said. "I wanted to do something that felt alive, you know? Not just another restaurant. Something that made people feel like they'd discovered something real."

Emma looked at the photos, then at him. "Why are you showing me this?"

"Because you're an architect who just spilled coffee on my blueprints, and I think that might be the least convenient thing that's happened to me all week, which means it might be the best."

"That's not logic."

"No," he agreed. "It's not."

The server brought his coffee. Emma watched him wrap his hands around the mug the way she'd been doing with hers, like it was something that could anchor you to the moment.

"I should give you my number," she heard herself say. "For the blueprints. I could redraw them. Or at least help you figure out what you want before you redraw them yourself."

"Is that professional courtesy or are you asking me on a date?" he asked.

Emma opened her mouth. Closed it. "I don't know yet."

Marcus smiled again. "I like that answer."

He pulled out his phone. Their fingers brushed when she took it to enter her number, and she felt something spark—not metaphorical, not the kind of thing you describe in romance novels. Something real. Something that made her skin feel too small for her body.

"Emma," he said, reading the contact name back to himself. "Emma Chen, architect."

"Marcus Rivera," she said, trying it out. "Restaurant owner and believer in cosmic coffee spills."

"It's a working theory."

When she left the cafe twenty minutes later, she didn't feel jittery about the tech company presentation anymore. She felt something else entirely. Something that tasted like coffee and possibility and the particular vertigo of having your day completely rewritten by someone's smile.

Her phone buzzed as she reached her car. A text from an unknown number: *I'm going to need those blueprints. And also, are you free for dinner?*

Emma sat in the driver's seat and smiled at the water.
