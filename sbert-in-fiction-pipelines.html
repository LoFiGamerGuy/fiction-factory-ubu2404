A Technical Analysis

Sentence Transformers in Long‑Form Fiction Pipelines

Where embedding-based similarity earns its keep in LLM novel-writing systems, where it doesn't, and what to layer alongside it for measurable anti-AI-voice gains.

Audience  ·  Pipeline architects, ML engineers, editorial-tool builders Status  ·  For evaluation & inclusion

Executive Summary

Sentence Transformers (SBERT) are useful in long-form fiction generation pipelines, but they are the wrong tool for the central problem most teams are trying to solve: making generated prose stop sounding like AI.

The reason is structural. SBERT models are explicitly trained to collapse over surface variation and concentrate signal in semantic meaning. AI tells live almost entirely in surface features — token distributions, syntactic patterns, lexical tics, punctuation density, rhythm. Embedding similarity is, by design, blind to exactly the signal that matters.

This document separates the genuinely useful SBERT applications from the layer that actually moves prose quality, and closes with a recommended tiering for integration. The underlying design principle assumed throughout: LLMs generate, code measures, code decides. Both layers described here are deterministic measurement and decision systems sitting around the generative agents, not inside them.

§ 01 The Core Problem

When practitioners say a generated novel "sounds like AI," they are pointing at a specific cluster of artifacts — not a general aesthetic complaint. The cluster is identifiable, measurable, and to a large extent controllable. Knowing what is in it determines where the work of mitigation should sit in the pipeline.

The cluster typically includes:

  • High em-dash density and stereotyped sentence patterns — "It's not X, it's Y."
  • Lexical tics: delve, tapestry, whispers, navigate the landscape, in the realm of, a testament to.
  • Low sentence-length variance; uniform pacing within paragraphs.
  • Reflexive parenthetical asides and appositive over-explanation.
  • Participial-phrase-before-comma sentence openers, repeated across pages.
  • Semantic repetition — the same idea returning in different surface clothing, often unnoticed by the model.
  • Uncanny "explainer voice" intrusions in close-third narration.
  • Excessive coordination via and, but, yet where a real prose stylist would vary structure.

All of these are surface-feature phenomena. They are detectable, measurable, and constrainable with classical NLP — but only if the measurement system is looking at the right level of abstraction.

A pipeline that addresses these only through prompt engineering will plateau. A pipeline that wraps generation in a deterministic measurement-and-revision loop can keep improving.

§ 02 Why SBERT Is the Wrong Tool Here

SBERT models are trained on objectives — natural language inference, paraphrase identification, semantic textual similarity — that explicitly reward representations invariant to surface differences. A well-trained sentence embedding will place these two sentences close together in vector space:

Demonstration — Same Meaning, Opposite Voice

The old man walked slowly down the path.
With measured, deliberate steps, the elderly gentleman traversed the winding trail — a silent testament to the weight of years carried in his frame.

The first reads like working novelist prose. The second is AI-slop. Their cosine distance is small. That is the model behaving correctly for its training objective, and exactly wrong for AI-tell detection.

Any pipeline that uses sentence-embedding similarity as its primary voice-quality signal will systematically miss the artifacts it most needs to catch. Reframing the problem as "build a better embedding model" usually fails too — the surface features carry low semantic information by definition, so they get squeezed out of any embedding optimized for meaning.

The artifacts that make LLM prose recognizable as LLM prose are surface-feature artifacts — and embedding models are explicitly trained to be invariant to surface features.

This is not an argument against SBERT. It is an argument for placing it in the parts of the pipeline where its strengths apply.

§ 03 Where SBERT Adds Real Value

Four high-leverage applications, in rough order of payoff.

3.1Semantic Repetition Detection

Long-form LLM generation has a characteristic failure mode: the same idea or image returns multiple times across a manuscript in different surface clothing. A character "feels the weight of expectation" in chapter 3, then "carries the burden of what's expected of him" in chapter 11, then "shoulders the load of what others demand" in chapter 19. Surface-level deduplication cannot see this. Stylometry cannot see this. The author or editorial reader notices it on a re-read and finds it embarrassing.

SBERT is the right tool. Embed every sentence in the manuscript, cluster on cosine distance, surface any cluster of size > k with sentences drawn from different chapters or scenes. A reviewer agent or human editor then judges whether the repetition is deliberate (a motif) or an unconscious tic. This single application alone tends to justify the SBERT integration.

3.2Cross-Encoder Reranking of Candidate Revisions

When a drafter agent produces N candidate versions of a passage, a cross-encoder can score them against a target the team controls. Two viable target formulations:

  • Pairwise human-vs-AI discriminator. Train a cross-encoder on (human-novel-prose, AI-novel-prose) pairs assembled from public-domain literature versus model outputs. Use it as a reranker: prefer candidates the discriminator scores as more human-like.
  • Style-target matching. Given a target voice (excerpts from a reference author or established corpus), score candidates against that target. Cross-encoders score better than bi-encoders here because they attend across both sequences simultaneously.

Both formulations require training data, which is a real cost. They also do not replace the stylometric layer described later — they sit on top of it as a softer, learned signal.

3.3RAG into the Editorial Agent

An editorial agent benefits from being conditioned on the specific craft references most relevant to the passage under review. A pipeline that retrieves generic style guidance into the editorial pass underperforms one that retrieves passage-conditioned guidance.

Implementation sketch: index the style and voice profile corpus — curated author exemplars, craft notes, voice profile rubrics — with SBERT. At edit time, embed the current passage and retrieve the most semantically similar items from the index. Pass those retrievals into the editorial agent's context. The editorial pass is then critiquing this scene against directly comparable examples, not against a generic "good prose" prior.

This is a standard RAG pattern and the highest-yield non-novel use of SBERT in fiction pipelines.

3.4Scene and Chapter Coherence Drift

Embed each scene or chapter as a pooled or summarized vector. Track cosine drift across the manuscript. Use as a continuity signal:

  • Does the climactic chapter sit in a semantic neighborhood consistent with the setup chapters?
  • Where are the discontinuities, and are they intentional?
  • Has a subplot or thematic thread drifted out of the cosine neighborhood it occupied in earlier chapters?

This is a coarse signal, but it surfaces structural problems that are otherwise expensive to detect. Pair it with an explicit scene-and-chapter outline tracker for best results.

§ 04 What Actually Drives Anti-AI-Voice Gains

This is the layer that should sit closest to the drafter agent's output, before any embedding-based pass runs. All of it is classical NLP and classical stylometry. None of it requires neural models, though some components benefit from them.

4.1Function-Word Frequency & Burrows' Delta

Function words — articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliaries, pronouns — carry the strongest authorial fingerprint in stylometry. The classical Burrows' Delta measure compares a candidate passage to a target corpus on the frequency distribution of the top N function words and returns a distance.

Use a target corpus drawn from the author voice the project is trying to evoke — public-domain reference authors, the team's own prior accepted work, a curated corpus blend. Score every chapter (and ideally every scene) on Delta against the target. Surface outliers for revision. This single technique is the most underused control in current LLM fiction pipelines.

4.2N-Gram Blacklists

Maintain a versioned blacklist of model-specific tics. Examples that recur across major models in 2025–26 production output:

  • delve / delves / delving
  • tapestry of
  • navigate the landscape
  • a testament to
  • in the realm of
  • whispers of
  • the weight of
  • speaks volumes
  • underscores the importance
  • navigating the complexities
  • rich tapestry
  • at its core

The blacklist is not a hard ban — it is a budget. Allow each entry M occurrences per 10k words and surface violations for revision. Keep the list under version control; tics evolve as base models update.

4.3Surface-Feature Distributions

Compute and constrain:

  • Sentence length distribution — mean, variance, skew. AI prose tends toward low variance.
  • Em-dash, semicolon, parenthetical-aside density per page.
  • Type-token ratio across a sliding window — a proxy for lexical diversity.
  • Coordinator frequency (and, but, yet) per paragraph.
  • Adjective-stacking depth — how often does a noun arrive preceded by two or more adjectives.
  • Adverb density, especially -ly adverbs in dialogue tags.

Target distributions can be calibrated from the reference corpus. Deviations are surfaced as revision candidates, not auto-edited.

4.4Syntactic-Pattern Detection

Some patterns are AI tells regardless of frequency. POS-tag template matching catches these directly:

  • "It's not X, it's Y" and its variants.
  • Participial-phrase-before-comma sentence openers, repeated within a window.
  • Triadic intensifiers: "It was X, it was Y, it was Z."
  • "as if to [verb]" followed by abstraction.
  • "a kind of [abstract noun]" followed by qualifier.

Each pattern is implementable as a regex over POS-tagged text or as a small classifier. Keep them as discrete rules so they can be tuned per project.

4.5Perplexity Scoring Under a Base LLM

Counterintuitive but effective: AI-generated text typically has lower perplexity under the model class that generated it than human-written prose does. Score each sentence under a small open base model. Sentences sitting at perplexity floor are candidates for revision toward more "surprising" phrasings.

This is the underlying signal behind several public AI-detection tools, with the same caveats about evasion and false positives — but used as a revision signal inside a pipeline (not a binary classifier on outside text) it works well.

4.6Rhythm & Cadence

Optional but high-value. Syllable counts, stress patterns, and clause-boundary placement carry the music of prose. Tools like the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary plus light prosody analysis can score rhythmic monotony. A long passage where every sentence falls into the same iambic-leaning cadence reads as flat regardless of content quality.

§ 05 Recommended Layering

A pipeline that adopts both layers cleanly should structure them as follows.

Tier 01

Stylometric Measurement Layer — required

Runs against every drafter output before any other pass. Cheap, deterministic, fast.

  • N-gram tic budget
  • Burrows' Delta against target voice corpus
  • Surface-feature distribution constraints
  • Syntactic-pattern detection
  • Perplexity scoring under a base reference model

Violations are surfaced to a reviser agent (or back to the drafter) with the specific measurements that failed. The agent revises. The measurements run again. The loop terminates when all measurements pass thresholds.

Tier 02

Embedding Layer — high value, lower priority

Runs at scene, chapter, or manuscript boundaries.

  • Semantic repetition clustering across the manuscript
  • RAG retrieval into the editorial agent
  • Scene and chapter coherence drift monitoring
  • Cross-encoder reranking of candidate revisions, where training data is available
Tier 03

Human or Senior-Agent Review — always

Both layers above are filters. Neither replaces a final editorial pass. Their job is to make sure that pass is spent on craft and story, not on cleaning up mechanical AI tells.

§ 06 Implementation Notes

A few practical points that recur across teams building this.

6.1Calibration corpus matters more than algorithm choice

Burrows' Delta against a poorly chosen reference corpus produces worse results than a simpler measure against a well-chosen one. Spend time on the reference corpus — multiple authors blended for a target voice, public-domain where possible, ideally with the team's own prior accepted work folded in.

6.2Keep the measurement layer interpretable

When a chapter fails, the reviser agent (or human) needs to know why: which tic exceeded its budget, which surface metric drifted, which syntactic pattern repeated. A black-box quality score is much less useful than a structured report of specific violations.

6.3Version the rules

AI tells drift as base models update. The n-gram blacklist that mattered in early 2024 has partially shifted by mid-2026. Treat the rule set as a living artifact under version control.

6.4Do not over-constrain

Every metric has a target range, not a target value. A manuscript whose sentence-length variance exactly matches Hemingway's is not Hemingway — it is a strange artifact. Aim for distributions that fall within the band of accepted human prose for the target voice, not at any specific point.

6.5Embeddings are cheap but not free

A 50k-word manuscript has roughly 3–4k sentences. Embedding all of them with a modern SBERT model takes seconds on CPU and milliseconds on GPU. Repetition clustering across this scale is trivial. Do not over-engineer the infrastructure.

6.6Cross-encoders need training data the team has to assemble

If a project cannot commit to building a labeled pair corpus, skip the cross-encoder reranking application and rely on the stylometric layer plus retrieval. The other three SBERT applications work out of the box.

§ 07 Right Tool, Wrong Tool

A scannable mapping of concerns to layer assignments.

Concern Right Tool Wrong Tool
Lexical tics — delve, tapestry N-gram blacklist with budget SBERT similarity
Sentence rhythm monotony Length and cadence distributions SBERT similarity
AI-stereotyped syntactic patterns POS-template detection SBERT similarity
Author voice match Burrows' Delta on function words SBERT similarity
Lower-than-human perplexity Base-LLM perplexity scoring SBERT similarity
Semantic repetition across chapters SBERT clustering Stylometry
Passage-conditioned editorial retrieval SBERT RAG Stylometry
Scene coherence drift SBERT pooled-vector tracking Stylometry
Candidate revision ranking Cross-encoder, with training data Either layer alone

Closing Note

The strongest claim in this document is the simplest: the artifacts that make LLM prose recognizable as LLM prose are surface-feature artifacts, and embedding models are explicitly trained to be invariant to surface features. Any pipeline architecture that ignores this places its anti-AI-voice gating in the wrong layer.

SBERT belongs in a fiction pipeline. It just does not belong where most teams initially try to put it. Place the stylometric layer closest to the drafter, the embedding layer in the supporting roles where its strengths apply, and reserve human or senior-agent review for the work that neither layer can do.
